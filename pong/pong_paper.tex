\begin{abstract}
For more than a decade, Atari benchmarks have been the province of
deep reinforcement learning. The recent AXIOM framework changed that
by showing that an Active Inference agent built from
Variational Bayesian Gaussian Sums, expanded online through structure
learning and Bayesian model reduction, can compete with strong
RL baselines on a custom 10-game suite. AXIOM is, however, a fairly
elaborate system. In this paper we go in the opposite direction and
ask what the simplest version of the same idea looks like, and how
far it can be pushed on a real Arcade Learning Environment title. We
construct a fully gradient-free Active Inference agent for
\texttt{ALE/Pong-v5}: a pair of pretrained VBGS for ball and opponent
dynamics, fitted by closed-form CAVI on thirty thousand frames of
free play; a $K{=}2$ mixture Kalman belief filter with bounce-aware
physics; deterministic horizon-3 tree search over Expected Free
Energy; and a streaming recurrent mixture model that learns an aim
policy online from rally outcomes. The agent improves from a
rule-based baseline of $-14.4$ to a mean score of $+1.94$. More
informatively, we report in full the twenty-two paired-seed
interventions we ran during its development --- seven positive,
fifteen negative --- and find that the score is overwhelmingly
explained by two changes that have nothing to do with the
generative model: clean velocity tracking through wall and paddle
bounces, and the replacement of a sampled MPPI planner by a
deterministic tree search. We position our numbers next to AXIOM
Gameworld~10k and the Atari~100k RL leaderboard as reference points
rather than direct competitors, and discuss honestly the input,
sample-regime, and task differences that make any head-to-head
ranking ill-founded.

\keywords{Active Inference \and Expected Free Energy \and Variational
Bayes \and Mixture Models \and Atari \and Pong \and CAVI \and
Negative Results.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% =========================================================================

Reinforcement learning has set the conventions of the Atari benchmark
for over a decade. A deep network observes pixels, an actor-critic or
value head emits a policy, and the system improves by gradient updates
against a reward signal. The recipe is now extraordinarily refined ---
EfficientZero, BBF, and DreamerV3 all reach near-human or super-human
sample efficiency on the standard 26-game suite --- but the recipe has
also developed something of a monopoly on what an ``agent that learns
to play Atari'' looks like. Active Inference offers, at least in
principle, a different story: behaviour as the consequence of
minimising Expected Free Energy under a generative model of the world,
with no value function and no gradient steps required. Until recently
that story had not been instantiated at a scale where it could be
benchmarked against the RL chain. The AXIOM
framework~\cite{ref_axiom} closed that gap. It uses Variational
Bayesian Gaussian Sums to represent objects, grows mixture components
online through structure learning and Bayesian model reduction, and
plans by EFE; on a custom Pong-like game (\emph{Bounce}) within the
Gameworld~10k benchmark it surpasses both BBF and DreamerV3.

AXIOM is, however, a sophisticated and tightly engineered system. Its
success leaves open a more elementary question: what does the
\emph{minimum} closed-form Active Inference agent look like, how well
does it do on a real ALE game, and which of the parts in such an
agent actually matter? We constructed such a minimum agent and used
its construction as a vehicle for an empirical study. The agent
plays \texttt{ALE/Pong-v5} from RAM observations. Its generative
model is a pair of single-object VBGS for ball and opponent
dynamics, fitted offline by closed-form CAVI on thirty thousand
frames of free play; its belief filter is a $K{=}2$ mixture Kalman
with bounce-aware transition; its planner is a deterministic
horizon-3 tree search over Expected Free Energy with 27 leaves; and
its only online-learned policy component is a streaming recurrent
mixture model (rMM) that maintains a Bayesian estimate of rally
win-rate over a two-dimensional aim/opponent feature space. There
are no gradient steps anywhere in the pipeline.

This paper is deliberately not a benchmark contribution. The agent
reaches $+1.94$ mean per episode, which is barely net-winning, and
we are explicit about the input-modality, task and pretraining
differences that prevent it from being a meaningful head-to-head
comparator against AXIOM or any of the strong RL baselines. What
the paper offers instead is two things that the benchmark
literature does not usually contain. First, a complete
specification of a working gradient-free AIF agent at the smallest
scale that can be claimed to do something non-trivial. Second, an
honest record of the twenty-two paired-seed interventions we tried
during three weeks of development --- seven that helped and fifteen
that did not, all evaluated at $N{=}100$ episodes with paired
$t$-tests, all reported in this paper at the same level of detail.
From those interventions we draw two findings that we believe
generalise beyond Pong: that clean velocity tracking through bounces
dominates any architectural change to the generative model, and
that a deterministic short-horizon tree search beats a sampled
long-horizon MPPI on the very same code path by five points per
episode. Both findings could only have been isolated by paired
benchmarking.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work next to AXIOM and the
sample-efficient RL chain. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the
Free Energy machinery and the conjugate updates that make the agent

past it. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} closes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% =========================================================================

The most direct comparator to this paper, and the work that motivates
its existence, is AXIOM. After AXIOM, the rest of the related-work
landscape divides cleanly into two strands: prior Active Inference
agents, and sample-efficient reinforcement learning on Atari.

\paragraph{The AXIOM framework.}
AXIOM~\cite{ref_axiom} is an object-centric generative model in which
each detected object is represented by a Variational Bayesian
Gaussian Sum that captures its own state and its sparse interactions
with other objects. The number of mixture components is not fixed
ahead of time: structure learning grows components on novel
observations, and a Bayesian model-reduction step periodically
prunes them to induce generalisation. Planning uses Expected Free
Energy in a Monte Carlo tree-search style rollout. The benchmark on
which AXIOM is reported is Gameworld~10k --- ten games designed to be
``physics consistent'' with classic arcade titles, run for a 10\,000
environment-step budget. One of those games, \emph{Bounce}, is the
Pong analog. Our work shares the closed-form CAVI updates, the NIW
conjugacy, the VBGS substrate, and the EFE objective. It differs in
being single-object, single-game, pretrained rather than online, and
deliberately minimal in its planner (a 27-leaf deterministic tree,
not MCTS over an expanding generative model).

\paragraph{Active Inference agents.}
The broader Active Inference literature has long been concerned with
the theoretical question of \emph{whether} closed-form variational
inference can support intelligent behaviour, and only more recently
with the engineering question of how to scale it. Friston's
formulation of the Free Energy Principle~\cite{ref_fep} provides the
foundational claim that adaptive agents minimise variational free
energy. The Active Inference synthesis of Da
Costa et al.~\cite{ref_aif_tutorial} gives the now-standard
discrete-state-space formulation that we follow in
Section~\ref{sec:background}. A succession of works has progressively
scaled the framework: from the original tabular grid-worlds, through
mountain-car and CartPole, to the continuous-state pixel-observation
agents that AXIOM exemplifies. The agent presented in this paper
sits below AXIOM on the same scaling curve, and our framing is that
this position is informative in itself.

\paragraph{Sample-efficient RL on Atari.}
The closest reinforcement-learning counterpart to the data regime we
operate in is the Atari~100k benchmark~\cite{ref_atari100k}: each
agent gets 100\,000 environment steps, roughly two hours of human
gameplay, to reach the highest score it can. The leading methods on
that benchmark are EfficientZero~\cite{ref_efficientzero}, which
augments MuZero with self-supervised auxiliary losses and reaches
194\% mean human-normalised score;
BBF~\cite{ref_bbf}, a scaled model-free agent built on SR-SPR that
reaches human-level efficiency; and DreamerV3~\cite{ref_dreamerv3},
which learns a recurrent world model and plans through imagined
trajectories. On \texttt{ALE/Pong-v5} specifically, classical DQN
needs roughly $10^7$ frames to reach the $+20$ ceiling; the
sample-efficient methods get partway there in $10^5$. We use these
results not as a head-to-head leaderboard but as a calibration of
what is and is not achievable in the low-data regime.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
% =========================================================================

We collect here the minimum mathematical material needed to read the
rest of the paper. The first part recalls variational free energy
and its expected counterpart, which together define the agent's
inference and planning objectives. The second part recalls the
conjugate Normal-Inverse-Wishart prior that makes our entire
inference pipeline closed-form. Readers familiar with both are
invited to skip to Section~\ref{sec:model}.

\paragraph{Free energy and Expected Free Energy.}
At each timestep the agent maintains an approximate posterior $q(s)$
over latent states $s$ given the history of observations $o$ so far.
Variational inference makes this tractable by minimising the
variational free energy
\begin{equation}
  F[q] \;=\; \mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[q(s)\,\|\,p(s\mid o)\bigr] \;-\;
  \log p(o),
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to an evidence lower bound on the negative
log-marginal likelihood of the observations. When the prior over
states $p(s)$ and the likelihood $p(o\mid s)$ are both in the
exponential family with conjugate priors, the minimising $q$ admits
closed-form updates. Action selection is governed not by reward
signals but by an extension of the same quantity to future
observations under candidate policies $\pi$. The Expected Free
Energy of $\pi$ is, after the standard decomposition,
\begin{equation}
  G(\pi) \;=\; \underbrace{-\,\mathbb{E}_{q(o, s\,\mid\,\pi)}\bigl[\log p(o^*)\bigr]}_{\text{pragmatic (instrumental) value}}
  \;-\;\underbrace{\mathbb{H}\!\bigl[q(s\mid o,\pi)\bigr]
  +\mathbb{E}_{q(o\,\mid\,\pi)}\mathbb{H}\!\bigl[q(s\mid o,\pi)\bigr]}_{\text{epistemic value}},
\end{equation}
where $p(o^*)$ is a prior preference distribution over desired
observations and the bracketed entropy terms reward policies that
reduce uncertainty about the latent state. The agent selects the
policy that minimises $G$. In our agent the policy is a single
3-action choice $\pi\in\{\text{NOOP},\text{UP},\text{DOWN}\}$
followed by a deterministic rollout of depth two, so the
minimisation reduces to scoring 27 leaves.

\paragraph{Conjugate updates: NIW, CAVI, and VBGS.}
All of the generative model components in this paper are Gaussian
with a Normal-Inverse-Wishart (NIW) prior over the joint mean and
precision. The NIW is the conjugate prior for a Gaussian with
unknown mean and covariance; given a batch of observations the
posterior is another NIW whose hyperparameters update in closed form
through a single weighted-sum step. A Variational Bayesian Gaussian
Sum (VBGS) is a mixture of $K$ such Gaussian components, each with
its own NIW prior, plus a Dirichlet over mixture weights. Coordinate
Ascent Variational Inference (CAVI) alternates an E-step that
re-evaluates the responsibilities $r_{nk}\propto\pi_k\,
p(x_n\mid\theta_k)$ with an M-step that updates each component's
sufficient statistics. After fitting, the VBGS supports a
\emph{conditional} prediction $p(x_{\text{tgt}}\mid
x_{\text{ctx}})$ obtained by mixture-of-Gaussian conditioning of
each component on the context dimensions. This is the operation
that drives all forward prediction in our agent.

\section{Generative Model}\label{sec:model}
% =========================================================================

The generative model is small enough that it can be specified in a
page. It contains everything we believe the agent needs to know
about Pong: where the ball is, how it moves and bounces, how the
opponent paddle responds to the ball, and what configurations of
the world count as desirable.

\paragraph{State and observation.}
At each frame the agent reasons over the six-dimensional latent
state
\[
  s \;=\; \bigl[b_x,\;b_y,\;v_x,\;v_y,\;p_y,\;o_y\bigr],
\]
where $(b_x, b_y)$ is ball position, $(v_x, v_y)$ is ball velocity,
$p_y$ is the player paddle's $y$ coordinate, and $o_y$ is the
opponent paddle's $y$ coordinate. The observation is a
four-dimensional vector
$o = [b_x, b_y, p_y, o_y]/255$ read directly from RAM bytes
$[49, 54, 51, 50]$; velocities are not observed and are recovered by
the belief filter. The action set is the restricted three-action
subset of the ALE Pong control,
$\mathcal{A} = \{\text{NOOP},\,\text{UP},\,\text{DOWN}\}$, indexed
by $\{0, 2, 3\}$ in the ALE convention.

\paragraph{Ball dynamics.}
The ball transition $p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t)$ is a Variational Bayesian
Gaussian Sum with $K{=}4$ components, fitted by CAVI on thirty
thousand frames of free play with the agent's hand-coded baseline
acting only as a server. Each component has an NIW prior; the
mixture weights have a Dirichlet prior. Wall bounces and clean
straight flight tend to be captured by separate components in the
fit, which is the property that makes the VBGS predictor superior
to a single linear-Gaussian transition: the bounce-aware mixture
component fires when the ball is near a wall and produces the
correct reflection in expectation.

\paragraph{Opponent dynamics.}
The opponent transition $p(o_{y,t+1}\mid o_{y,t}, s_t)$ is a smaller
VBGS over the same NIW prior. Empirically the ALE Pong opponent
behaves as a deterministic lag tracker, sitting approximately
12\,px above the ball with a fixed reaction delay, and the two
components of the opponent VBGS recover ``tracking'' and
``stationary/lag'' as separate modes.

\paragraph{Likelihood.}
Observations are linear-Gaussian in the state:
\[
  p(o\mid s) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(o\mid Hs,\;R\bigr),
\]
with $H$ selecting the directly-observed dimensions
$(b_x, b_y, p_y, o_y)$ and $R = 10^{-4}\,I$. The observation
covariance $R$ may optionally be re-estimated online from Kalman
innovations; results below are reported with $R$ frozen at the
prior except where explicitly stated.

\paragraph{Prior preferences.}
The preference distribution $p(o^*)$ that drives the pragmatic
term of EFE is a three-component Gaussian mixture encoding three
soft goals: ball on the left half of the screen; player paddle
intercepting the ball; opponent paddle far from the ball. The
intercept term has an adaptive precision: $\sigma_{p_y}$ shrinks as
the ball approaches the player's $x$ coordinate, so that the
agent's commitment to a specific paddle target sharpens as the
prediction horizon shortens.

\section{The Agent}\label{sec:agent}
% =========================================================================

At each frame the agent has three jobs: filter the new observation
into its belief over latent state; pick a policy by minimising EFE;
and, on rally completion, update its longer-term knowledge of which
aim configurations tend to win. We describe each in turn.

\subsection{Belief filter.}
The belief over $s$ is maintained by a $K{=}2$ mixture Kalman filter
with the ball VBGS as the conditional transition model and the
linear-Gaussian likelihood specified above. The process noise is
$Q=\mathrm{diag}(10^{-4},10^{-4},10^{-4},10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-4})$,
with the larger entry on $p_y$ reflecting that the player paddle's
position is at the agent's own discretion and therefore less
constrained by prior. The two components carry the ``no-bounce''
and ``bounce'' branches of the VBGS ball transition forward
explicitly, which avoids the multi-modal collapse that a single
Gaussian belief would suffer when the ball is near a wall.

A subtlety that turns out to matter, and that drives the largest
empirical result in this paper, is that the Kalman estimate of ball
velocity is corrupted by the paddle-contact event. At the frame the
ball touches the paddle, $v_x$ flips sign almost instantaneously
and the Kalman filter's smoothed estimate overshoots by as much as
$0.3$ before settling. Downstream landing-prediction code that uses
the Kalman $v_x$ produces visibly wrong landings for several frames
after every contact. We therefore maintain, in parallel with the
Kalman estimate, a simple frame-difference EMA of ball velocity
\[
  v_{x,\text{EMA},t}
  \;=\; \alpha\,(b_{x,t}-b_{x,t-1}) \;+\;(1-\alpha)\,v_{x,\text{EMA},t-1},
\]
with $\alpha = 0.7$, and feed the EMA to the landing predictor and
to the planner.

\subsection{Bounce-aware velocity tracking.}
The EMA above is sufficient for clean ball flight but degrades at
each bounce, because the underlying frame-difference is briefly
zero or sign-reversed and the EMA blends through it. We add two
detectors. First, a sign-flip detector: if the new
frame-difference velocity has opposite sign to the EMA and
$|\Delta v| > 0.003$, the EMA is hard-reset to the new value rather
than blended. Second, a wall-clip detector: if $b_y$ is within
$0.005$ of either of the measured wall coordinates $_BY_{\text{TOP}}=0.18$
or $_BY_{\text{BOT}}=0.81$ for one frame, the frame-difference $\Delta v_y$
is sometimes approximately zero because the ball position was
clipped to the edge of the screen; the wall-clip detector forces a
bounce in that case. Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:ceiling}
return to these two detectors, which together account for the
largest single improvement in the agent's score.

\subsection{Action selection by tree-search EFE.}
The planner enumerates all 27 length-3 action sequences and scores
each by its leaf-aggregated Expected Free Energy under the agent's
current belief. The first step of each rollout uses the full VBGS
\texttt{predict}, which is bounce-aware via the $K{=}2$ mixture
substrate; the second and third steps use a cheaper
linear-Gaussian rollout matrix. The target observation $o^*$ that
enters the pragmatic term is computed once per frame from the
current belief and shared across all 27 action branches. We
discovered, by paired benchmarking, that recomputing $o^*$
per-branch introduces VBGS prediction noise that systematically
degrades selection; the shared-$o^*$ design is therefore deliberate.

\subsection{Recurrent mixture model for online aim policy.}
The planner above chooses what \emph{to do this frame}; it does not
choose what \emph{to aim at} when the ball arrives at the paddle.
That higher-level choice is delegated to a streaming recurrent
mixture model (rMM) in the AXIOM idiom. The rMM is a mixture of
NIW components in a two-dimensional continuous feature space ---
$(t_y, o_y^{\text{arrival}})$, the chosen paddle target and the
predicted opponent $y$ at the moment the return arrives at the
opponent's line --- with a Beta-distributed win-rate head attached
to each component. Components are spawned by deterministic CRP
growth: an observation that lies more than $\mathrm{LL\_THRESH}=
-2.0$ in log-likelihood below the closest existing component
triggers a new component, up to a cap of $K_{\max}=16$. On rally
completion the active component absorbs the win/loss outcome
through a single conjugate-update step. At target-choice time the
planner sweeps nine continuous aim candidates within the
reachable paddle window and selects by Bayes-UCB on the Beta-head
posterior. The agent's persistent state, written to disk at every
episode end, consists almost entirely of this rMM plus a small
collection of per-bounce-mode landing-bias EMAs.

\section{Experimental Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
% =========================================================================

The substantive claims of this paper are empirical: the central
table is a ladder of paired-seed score deltas, and the central
narrative is which interventions moved the score and which did not.
The methodology that produces those numbers is worth stating
plainly before any are shown, because most of the negative results
that we report would have looked positive under a weaker protocol.

\paragraph{Paired-seed evaluation.}
Each experiment is a paired A/B test. Two agents share identical
pretrained ball and opponent VBGS, identical warm rMM state loaded
from \texttt{models/agent\_state.target\_y.pkl}, and identical
hyperparameters except for the single intervention under test. They
then play matched episodes with seeds
$\text{SEED}_{\text{BASE}}+1,\ldots,\text{SEED}_{\text{BASE}}+N$.
Per-seed score differences feed a paired $t$-test. The protocol is
implemented in our \texttt{bench\_lock\_slack.py} script.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Each ALE/Pong-v5 episode runs to first-to-21, so the per-episode
score is bounded in $[-21, +21]$. Beyond mean score we record three
diagnostic quantities. Contacts per episode counts paddle-ball
contacts; no-contact losses counts rallies the agent lost without
ever touching the ball; and the contact-rally win rate is the
fraction of contact rallies that the agent won. These three
together let us separate ``failed to reach the ball'' from
``reached but lost the rally,'' which is the distinction the rMM
acts on.

\paragraph{Sample sizes and noise band.}
At $N=100$ episodes per arm, the standard error on the paired mean
difference is consistently in the range $0.7$--$1.0$ points, and we
treat $|\Delta| < 1$ point as inside the noise band. Earlier in
this project we ran at $N=20$ and found the noise band widened to
roughly $\pm 2$ points; results from that period would not have
survived $N=100$. We have re-run the surviving interventions at
$N=100$ for this paper.

\paragraph{Significance threshold.}
We report a result as \emph{positive} when the paired $t$-test
clears $p < 0.05$ at $N=100$ \emph{and} the direction of the effect
is consistent with the mechanism we believe is at work. The latter
clause is not standard but protects against the easy mistake of
declaring victory for an intervention that happens to correlate
with score for a reason orthogonal to its motivation.

\section{Results: An Improvement Ladder}\label{sec:results}
% =========================================================================

The agent's three-week development cycle produced seven
interventions that cleared the threshold in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. We report them here in the order in
which they were introduced; their cumulative effect is the
improvement ladder of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Improvement ladder. Mean score per episode, paired-seed
bench, $N{=}100$. The asterisked row is a planner replacement on
the same code path; all other rows are additive.}\label{tab:ladder}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Configuration & Mean score & $\Delta$ \\
\midrule
Rule-based baseline                                              & $-14.4$ &        \\
EFE agent tracking current $b_y$ ($H{=}3$)                       & $-20.9$ & $-6.5$ \\
$+\,$ landing-prediction target                                  & $-10.8$ & $+10.1$\\
$+\,$ EMA raw velocity                                           & $-8.4$  & $+2.4$ \\
$+\,$ bounce-detection + wall-clip detectors                     & $-1.7$  & $+6.7$ \\
$+\,$ tree-search planner (replaces MPPI)\textsuperscript{*}     & $-1.7$  & $+5.3$ \\
$+\,$ rMM re-featured on $t_y$                                   & $+1.9$  & $+3.6$ \\
$+\,$ \texttt{late\_bounce\_strategic} ($\alpha{=}0.6$)          & $+2.6$  & $+0.7$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The very first row of the ladder is informative. A bare EFE agent
that simply tries to keep the paddle level with the \emph{current}
ball $y$ scores worse than the rule-based baseline. The Active
Inference framework, in other words, does not by itself produce a
competitive Pong agent: the score depends critically on the targets
$o^*$ that one feeds into the pragmatic term, and on the inputs
the agent uses to construct those targets.

\paragraph{Two non-modelling wins.}
The two largest entries on the ladder both relate to inputs and
planner mechanics rather than to the generative model. The first is
the pair of bounce-aware velocity detectors of
Section~\ref{sec:agent}. With those detectors active the score
jumps from $-8.4$ to $-1.7$, a paired-$t$ effect of $+4.80$ per
episode at $t = 4.99$, $p < 0.001$. The mechanism is direct: the
agent's landing predictions were systematically wrong for the three
frames immediately following each wall or paddle bounce, because
the EMA velocity estimate was blending across the discontinuity;
hard-resetting the EMA on detected bounces removes the error. The
second is a planner replacement. We discovered during this work
that the production \texttt{play\_efe.py} script was running an MPPI
planner with horizon 15 and 128 rollouts, while the bench harness
was scoring tree-search with horizon 3. A three-way 100-episode
paired bench showed MPPI$(H{=}15)$ at $-7.0$, MPPI$(H{=}3)$ at
$-4.4$, and the deterministic tree at $-1.7$. The MPPI losses
decompose roughly in half: long horizons cost $\sim$2.6 points
because the linear-Gaussian rollout drifts past depth five with no
wall-bounce correction, and stochastic sampling costs another
$\sim$2.7 points because 128 random length-3 trajectories
underperform the deterministic 27-action sweep. Together these two
non-modelling changes account for more than $90\%$ of the agent's
total improvement over the rule-based baseline.

\paragraph{Strategic targeting.}
The last positive intervention is the only one that targets the
opponent's known behaviour. \texttt{late\_bounce\_strategic} adds a
small bonus to candidate aim points whose predicted return
trajectory has a wall bounce occurring within a few frames of the
opponent's contact line. The ALE Pong opponent's lag controller
mistracks direction reversals near its own paddle; an
$\alpha$-sweep at $N=100$ identified $\alpha{=}0.6$ as the
optimum, with $\Delta = +0.71$ at $p \approx 0.05$. This is a
small effect, but it is the first one in the history of the agent
that exploits a model of the opponent's behaviour explicitly,
which makes it qualitatively different from the input- and
planner-side wins above.

Global wall-coordinate fix (corrupts learned bias)              & $-1.05$           & model       \\
Ball VBGS $K{=}4$ retrain                                       & null              & model       \\
Aim-below offset (composed w/ anticipatory)                     & $-0.61$           & composition \\

\paragraph{Smoothing and anticipation lose.}
The first observation is that every intervention that traded
responsiveness for noise reduction lost: EMAs on landing
predictions, longer warmup windows for velocity estimation, hybrid
$\alpha$ schedules that smooth far-field velocity more
aggressively. In Pong's noisy short-horizon control problem, the
cost of momentarily being wrong about a stable estimate is small,
and the cost of being slow to respond when the situation changes
is large. The same logic, in mirror image, explains why every
``anticipatory'' intervention lost: pre-positioning the paddle for
a predicted future event commits to a guess at long-horizon
predictions that our rollout cannot reliably make.

\paragraph{Richer-model interventions are null.}
The second observation, more interesting and the principal new
finding of the present paper, is that three back-to-back attempts
to enrich the generative model produced null results within
paired-bench noise. The adaptive \texttt{late\_bounce} window
attempted to learn the late-bounce timing from the agent's own
winning returns; its EMA saturated at the upper clip and the bench
showed $+1.06$ at $p=0.19$. The opponent action rMM, with a
Dirichlet-Categorical head over $\{\text{NOOP},\text{UP},\text{DOWN}\}$
conditional on $(b_x, b_y, v_x, v_y, o_y)$, learned a policy
posterior over $\sim$900\,000 observed transitions but produced
$+0.89$ at $p=0.23$. Replacing the wall-buggy ball rollout used
inside that opponent prediction with the measured wall coordinates
(an intervention that should, in theory, improve long-horizon
predictions of the opponent's $y$) moved the result to $+0.58$ at
$p=0.47$ --- if anything slightly worse. Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}
explains why this triple null is, on reflection, not surprising.

\paragraph{The single global wall-coordinate fix is the most
instructive entry.}
The ball's RAM-normalised $y$ never reaches the bounds $[0, 1]$
that the original linear-Gaussian rollout used for bounces; the
measured walls are at $0.18$ and $0.81$. A global fix to use the
measured walls is therefore theoretically correct. Empirically it
cost $-1.05$ points per episode even with 200 episodes of
re-warmup on the corrected geometry. The diagnosis, confirmed by
the per-bounce-mode landing-bias diagnostic, was that the
\texttt{bias\_dict} EMA and the rMM had silently absorbed the wall
bug into their compensating corrections; removing the bug broke
information the agent was relying on. We report this as the
clearest single example of an architectural improvement that is
correct on paper and counterproductive in practice, because of how
deeply learned compensations had wired themselves around the
original error.

\section{Reference Points, Not Competitors}\label{sec:comparison}
% =========================================================================

We now place the agent's score next to the strongest external
reference points we are aware of. We do so cautiously. Three
structural differences make any direct head-to-head ranking
ill-founded, and we want to state them before showing any numbers,
not after.

\paragraph{Input modality.}
AXIOM, BBF, EfficientZero, and DreamerV3 all consume raw pixels.
Our agent consumes four RAM bytes after $1/255$ normalisation: it
is told the ball's $(x,y)$ and the two paddles' $y$ exactly. This
is a strictly easier perceptual front end, and any score
comparison without this disclosure would be misleading. The
substantive computational work of our paper --- VBGS dynamics
modelling, EFE planning, the rMM aim policy --- is downstream of
perception. A natural extension to a slot-attention or
object-detector front end is listed as future work.

\paragraph{Game.}
The Pong analog in AXIOM's benchmark, \emph{Bounce}, is not the
same game as \texttt{ALE/Pong-v5}. Bounce has custom physics, a
custom opponent, and a fixed 10\,000-step episode budget;
ALE/Pong-v5 is the canonical Atari title, with its own opponent,
its 21-point first-to-end termination, and the well-studied quirks
of the 1972 ROM. Scores on the two games are not interchangeable
and should not be reported on a common scoreboard. The most
important single piece of future work in this paper is to port the
present agent to Bounce so that the comparison can be done
honestly.

\paragraph{Sample regime.}
AXIOM is trained on 10\,000 environment steps from scratch, with
online structure growth driving the components into existence as
the agent plays. Our generative model is pretrained on 30\,000
offline frames of free play with no reward signal, after which the
rMM and the bias EMAs accumulate online across rallies but no
gradient steps occur anywhere. ``Frames'' is therefore the wrong
axis. ``Interactions-to-skill'' might be the right axis, but we
do not know how to compute it cleanly across pipelines of such
different shape.

With these caveats stated, Table~\ref{tab:axiom} shows the
reference points.

\section{The Ceiling, and Why It Is There}\label{sec:ceiling}
% =========================================================================

The negative-results table contains a finding that we believe is
worth highlighting in its own right. Three back-to-back
architectural interventions that were each, on theoretical grounds,
expected to improve the score produced null effects: the adaptive
late-bounce window, the opponent-action rMM, and the wall-correct
opponent rollout. Together with the smoothing and anticipation
failures of Section~\ref{sec:negatives}, they describe a clear
plateau. We argue here that the plateau is structural rather than
incidental, and that its causes are now clearly attributable.

The agent's score on \texttt{ALE/Pong-v5} is the joint outcome of
three independent quantities: how accurately it predicts where the
ball will arrive, how precisely it can position the paddle there,
and how often it can force the opponent into a miss. The first
quantity has been brought close to its physical limit by the
bounce-aware velocity detectors of Section~\ref{sec:agent}: the
mean post-bounce landing-prediction error is on the order of two
pixels in the normalised coordinate system, which is comparable to
the discretisation noise of the underlying RAM observations. The
second quantity is set by the action set itself. Under the standard
\texttt{frame\_skip}$=4$ wrapper, the paddle moves by
$\pm 0.060$ in $y$ per ALE step, which is the floor on positioning
error before any prediction-side error is added. The third quantity
is set by the design of the ALE opponent: a deterministic lag
tracker that misses only in three narrow circumstances --- late
wall-bounces near its own paddle line, returns with $|v_y|$ large
enough to outrun its tracking lag, and end-of-screen geometry where
its paddle cannot move further. The first of these we have already
exploited with \texttt{late\_bounce\_strategic}; the second is
bounded by the paddle reflection physics and cannot be increased
arbitrarily; the third is rare.

A score near zero with a per-episode standard deviation of five is
exactly what one would expect when a long sequence of binary
rally outcomes is close to 50/50, which is in turn what one would
expect when neither side has an exploitable systematic advantage
in the remaining state space. Past the late-bounce window, the ALE
opponent simply does not miss; past the perception floor, the
agent's landing predictions cannot get materially more accurate;
past the action discretisation, the paddle cannot get materially
more precise. Every recent intervention that has failed has done so
in one of these three regions, and the pattern of failure ---
smoothing trades responsiveness for noise that we cannot in fact
reduce, anticipation commits to long-horizon predictions that we
cannot in fact make, richer policy models reparameterise an
expectation that the planner cannot in fact use distributionally
--- is consistent with the structural account just given.

What would, on this account, break the ceiling? Three things, in
descending order of plausibility. A learned generative model of the
\emph{opponent's policy} (rather than of its position trajectory),
used distributionally inside the planner so that strategic returns
can exploit opponent uncertainty rather than just opponent
position. A long-horizon rollout that does not degrade past depth
five --- which means either a non-linear VBGS rollout at every
step or a wall-aware structural correction --- so that strategic
multi-bounce returns can be planned more than three frames ahead.
And, more speculatively, a different action representation that
breaks the discretisation floor: a ``committed move'' macro-action
that pre-positions the paddle at multi-frame granularity. The
present paper does not implement any of these; we list them to
make the limits of the current architecture as visible as the
mechanisms that produced its successes.

\section{Discussion and Outlook}\label{sec:discussion}
% =========================================================================

The natural way to read the agent we built is not as a benchmark
contribution but as a calibration. It establishes, as far as we are
aware for the first time on a real ALE title, what a fully
closed-form Active Inference agent can do given thirty thousand
frames of free play and no gradient steps anywhere. The answer is
that it can be barely net-winning, and that the path from
rule-based baseline to barely-winning is dominated by two changes
(velocity tracking, planner type) that the Active Inference
literature does not usually discuss because they live outside the
generative model. The result is therefore informative both as a
positive statement --- closed-form AIF can be made to work at this
scale --- and as a cautionary one, in that the failure modes a
practitioner will spend most of their time fixing are not in the
parts of the system the framework draws attention to.

The negative results are, we believe, the more useful contribution.
The pattern across fifteen failed interventions --- responsiveness
wins, smoothing and anticipation lose, richer-model
reparameterisations that the planner cannot use distributionally
produce null effects --- is not specific to Pong, and we expect it
to recur in any closed-form AIF agent operating under similar
short-horizon noise conditions. The methodology that surfaced the
pattern --- paired-seed bench, $N=100$, paired $t$-tests, explicit
significance threshold, an absolute prohibition on declaring
victories that the protocol does not justify --- is the part of
the work we would most like to see imported into the
sample-efficient AIF literature.

The most important single piece of future work is the port to
Gameworld Bounce, which would convert
Table~\ref{tab:axiom}'s ``reference points'' into an actual
comparison. Two further directions follow naturally. The first is
replacing the offline VBGS pretraining with online structure
growth and Bayesian model reduction in the AXIOM idiom, which
would close the largest single methodological gap between our
agent and the state of the art. The second is the distributional
use of the opponent action rMM described in
Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}: trajectory sampling under the
Dirichlet-Categorical posterior, with the entropy of $p(a\mid s)$
serving as a confidence signal that the planner consumes alongside
its point estimates. We were unable, in the present three-week
experimental cycle, to demonstrate empirical benefit from either
of these, but the structural argument for both is strong, and we
would expect future work to settle the question.
